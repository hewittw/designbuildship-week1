Before The Brief

Why I actually did this.

My goal this week was to see how wide and distinct a range of genuinely creative websites I could get out of AI — not how many, how different. I used AI all summer too, but for backend code headed to production, where the whole point is correctness and restraint. This was the opposite exercise: push AI design output to its actual limit and see where it breaks.

"Don't worry about the last 10%."— Professor Andre, advice I took literally

I took that seriously. I wasn't chasing exact copy text or small style nitpicks on any individual page — what I was optimizing for was the overall spread of design, and that's exactly where I put the effort. Seeing my own resume reshaped into a working Instagram profile, or into Netflix, from nothing but prompting, genuinely blew my mind. My personal favorite out of all 25 is the SpaceX mission-control launch page (v25) — it's the most creative, most different thing in the whole set, and it's a page I never would have thought to build, or had time to build by hand, before AI made it a real option in an afternoon.

Worth saying how I actually ran this, since it's most of why it moved as fast as it did: I had several terminals open at once, each commanding at least 20 sub-agents, which was extremely effective for ripping out first drafts. Once pages existed, I switched modes — I filed GitHub issues as I found problems, kept one terminal with its own subagents working through that issue queue in real time while I kept filing more, in parallel with a second terminal I drove myself for the more specific, nuanced changes that needed a closer hand. My actual starting process was slow: two full starter folders, long written descriptions of my vision for both the personal page and the club lacrosse page, plus folders of real images and documents to pull from. That upfront foundation is what let everything after it move quickly — and again, the whole way through, I was optimizing for design range, not for finite details or exact wording.

The Brief

25 pages. One stack. Judge the page, not the pitch.

The assignment: at least 25 distinct landing-page versions plus one gallery that tells the story of the process, built in plain HTML, CSS, and optional vanilla JavaScript only — no frameworks, no build step, no external APIs, no data storage — deployed from a GitHub repo to Vercel. Explore wide first (v01–v10), steer toward what's working (v11–v19), then converge and refine the winning direction (v20–v25). Every version had to be a genuinely different creative bet — layout, audience, mood, era, tone — not a palette swap.

We ended up building 37 pages across two sites instead of one 25-page set: a personal portfolio for Hewitt, and a recruiting one-pager for UChicago Men's Club Lacrosse. Both are real — the lacrosse content is grounded in the club's actual RSO registration documents and a real, verified 2024 GLLL Division 2 championship (11–0), not invented copy.

Phase 1 — Explore

Prove the ceiling before picking a direction.

Before committing to any landing-page concept, we built hw1/ as a pure technique study: ten self-contained pages proving out the most ambitious scroll and interaction techniques achievable under the "no frameworks, no CDNs, not even Google Fonts" constraint — a scroll-scrubbed canvas icosahedron, native CSS animation-timeline: view() kinetic type, a vertical-to-horizontal scroll hijack, a hand-written WebGL shader, an SVG path-morph, a magnetic cursor, a CSS 3D diorama, the View Transitions API, velocity-reactive particles, and a tile-assembly grid. Content didn't matter yet — only whether the technique was honestly achievable with zero dependencies.

"i don't think a lot of them work as intended — please check your work."— the single most useful piece of feedback in the whole project

That pushback was correct: 4 of the 10 pages had severe rendering bugs with zero console errors, because the bugs were CSS layout issues, not JS exceptions. Checking the console is necessary but nowhere near sufficient for scroll-driven pages — verification from that point on meant real screenshots at multiple scroll depths, every time.

overflow-x:hidden + position:sticky

Setting overflow-x:hidden on html/body silently breaks the containing block for any descendant using position:sticky — pinned sections rendered completely blank through their entire middle.

height:100% on body

Caps the whole document at exactly one viewport and makes it entirely unscrollable — one page never advanced past its first chapter no matter how far you scrolled.

Unscoped animation-timeline

animation-timeline: scroll(root) without a scoped range animates across the entire document's scroll distance, not the local pinned section — the effect barely progressed within its own visible area.

Unescaped apostrophes in JS

"doesn't", "isn't" inside single-quoted string literals silently broke the entire script on one page — the fix we now check for on every single build since.

With the ceiling proven, the real idea landed: reuse the ten techniques as templates and launch twelve parallel, context-isolated agents per site — personal site and lacrosse site — each given the full content brief, one specific direction, the known-bug list to avoid, and nothing else. Isolation was deliberate: no shared context between agents keeps divergent creative bets from bleeding into each other, which is the entire point of the explore phase.

Verification at this stage meant: grepping every output file for the known bug patterns first (2 of 12 personal pages had reintroduced the sticky/overflow bug anyway, despite being warned), running every inline script through a syntax check, then a full headless-browser pass checking console errors and screenshots at several scroll depths on every page. One near-miss: a coarse, evenly-spaced screenshot sweep made a page with long, unevenly-sized sections look broken — it wasn't; the samples had just landed on blank stretches by bad luck. Lesson: for uneven layouts, verify specific elements directly rather than trusting a fixed grid of scroll-fraction screenshots.

Before deploying, a real path bug nearly broke the whole thing: every page reached outside hw1/ via ../personal-materials/..., which only works locally — it's fatal once hw1/ is the deployed Vercel root, since nothing outside it ships. Fixed by copying every referenced asset inside hw1/ and repointing every path, then re-verifying by serving hw1/ itself as the HTTP root — exactly how Vercel serves it.

Phase 2 — Steer

Combine what's working. Fix what isn't, fast.

With 24 pages live, steering meant two things: deepening the strongest shared element across every version (the three project visualizations — satellite anomaly detection, LZW compression, a SQL join — all real, independently-implemented algorithms, never a shared static widget), and widening the personal site past its original 12 with six real app/brand clones: Instagram, iMessage, Apple Notes, Netflix, a Quizlet study-set flow, and an original "float while reading" ocean-waves direction. The thesis these pages prove: the resume content and the delivery vehicle are completely separable — the same fourteen facts can be a chat thread, a flashcard deck, or a product feed and still be the same resume.

One version got singled out for a dedicated second pass. User feedback on the ocean-waves page: "it needs to feel incredibly modern and sophisticated and that scrolling is immersive in the ocean." The first build only drifted from near-white to pale blue across the entire page — too subtle to register as a descent. The rebuild added eight real depth stages driving ~25 CSS color variables every frame, hand-rolled canvas caustics, marine-snow particles, and a glass depth gauge — the palette now genuinely travels from white foam to near-black by the footer. That's the steer phase in miniature: don't rebuild everything, isolate the one thing that isn't landing and go deep on exactly that.

This session's own fast-feedback loop was pure steering: inconsistent per-chapter coloring on one page unified to match the rest of the site; a confusing $ whoami label removed; a hero photo recentered after default cropping cut off the face on three different pages (fixed with object-position:50% 18% once we knew the source portrait's proportions); a mouse-tilt effect clamped after it could rotate a card off-screen; an assembly animation that exploded back apart on scroll wired to stay locked together instead. Small, specific, fast — exactly what steering should look like.

A QA pass this session also caught something structural rather than stylistic: 9 of the 18 personal pages had the contact email rendered as plain, unclickable text — or, on the terminal page, printed as inert output instead of a real link. All 12 lacrosse pages were already correct. Every instance is now a genuine mailto: link, verified in a real headless browser by actually finding the rendered anchor on each page, not just checking the source for the string.

Phase 3 — Refine

Raise the ceiling again, then lock the best of it in.

The last seven personal versions (v19–v25) were commissioned as one batch and built by seven parallel Opus agents, each given the complete content brief, the full known-bug checklist, and one wholly distinct creative mandate: a Five Nights at Freddy's security-office night shift, a New Zealand All Blacks matchday programme, a fully-functional Amazon product listing, a black-and-white darkroom contact sheet, a printed-circuit-board schematic you watch power on, a page rendered as live, correct, reversible binary, and a SpaceX-style mission-control launch. Every one of them still carries the exact same facts, the same two headshots, the same three dogs, and three independently-coded, actually-running algorithm demos — refine means getting precise about execution, not changing what's true.

Verification standard for this batch, enforced on every single agent: a Node syntax check on the extracted script, a real headless-Chromium pass with zero tolerance for console or page errors, every image checked for naturalWidth > 0 (catches silently-broken paths a console check misses), and — critically — every claimed interaction actually exercised and its resulting state change confirmed, not just "the button exists in the DOM." Two of the seven self-reported and fixed real issues mid-build without being asked: one made a too-subtle animation dramatically more visible after reviewing its own screenshots; another found and fixed a scroll-position drift bug in its own English/binary toggle before calling itself done.

With 25 personal pages and 12 lacrosse pages done, the gallery itself got refined to match: the thirteen strongest personal executions — six app/brand clones plus the seven final-batch themes — are featured up top; the original twelve explore-phase bets are archived at the bottom as "Personal — First Attempt," so the gallery's own layout now tells the explore→steer→refine story, not just links to it.

Final Choice — Personal Site

v25 · "Mission Control"

★ Final Pick

A scroll-driven SpaceX launch, told through the same resume.

personal-v25.html

Of all 25 personal versions, this is the one we'd actually ship. It's the single most technically ambitious piece of pure CSS/SVG/vanilla-JS choreography in the set — a sticky 360vh hero that genuinely flies a rocket through a countdown, gravity turn, MECO, stage separation, and second-stage ignition, all driven by scroll position, with a live telemetry HUD (speed, altitude, G-load) reacting the whole way.

  • Legible instantly. You don't need to discover a gimmick (like the binary-decode page) to feel the "wow" — it's in the first three seconds, on load.
  • Thematic unity. The hero's own "payload" project is the satellite telemetry anomaly detector — a real project of Hewitt's — so the launch theme isn't decorative, it's the actual subject matter talking about itself.
  • Reads as a real company's site. Confident type, disciplined one-accent-color palette, a HUD that looks like broadcast telemetry rather than a dashboard mockup.
  • Still fully resume-complete. Every job, the publication, all three algorithm demos, skills, and hobbies survive the theme intact — nothing was sacrificed for the spectacle.

Final Choice — Lacrosse Site

lax‑v10 · "Scandinavian Minimal"

★ Final Pick

The one that would actually convert a recruit.

lax-v10.html

Chosen by actually screenshotting the strongest candidates side by side — the ESPN-style broadcast scoreboard, the UChicago Athletics homage, the dark trophy-case museum, and this one — rather than picking from the gallery blurbs. This is the one that nails the brief's own refine-mode criteria: "built to the level of a real product landing page," precise about spacing, type scale, and wording.

  • Nike/Patagonia-grade restraint. Huge declarative type, one disciplined maroon accent, enormous negative space — nothing competes with the headline for attention.
  • Clearest single CTA on either site. "Sign up for spring" is unmissable and un-crowded, with a secondary "The 2024 season" link kept visually quiet.
  • Real information, real hierarchy. Breadcrumb nav, the real 11–0 record, a tightly cropped editorial championship photo — nothing invented, nothing decorative for its own sake.
  • Does the actual job. A recruiting page's only real success metric is "does a visitor sign up" — the flashier scoreboard and museum concepts are more fun to visit, but this is the one that would convert.

Lessons, Kept

What we'd tell the next person doing this.

  • No console errors is not "it works." CSS/layout bugs render silently blank. Verify with real screenshots at multiple scroll depths, and check specific elements directly on long or uneven pages rather than trusting evenly-spaced samples.
  • Isolate agents for divergent bets, ground them in one shared brief for convergent ones. The explore phase worked because twelve agents never saw each other's output. The steer/refine phases worked because every agent read the exact same resume facts and bug checklist.
  • Ground every fact in a real source. The lacrosse site's record, officers, and league are pulled from the club's actual RSO paperwork — not invented, and the full internal roster and personal emails were deliberately excluded per an explicit privacy boundary.
  • Deploy-path bugs hide until deploy. Relative paths that reach outside the project root work perfectly on a local machine and break completely the moment the folder becomes the actual Vercel root. Verify by serving the deploy root itself, locally, before trusting it.
  • A visible button that does nothing is worse than no button. The email-link audit this session found nine pages where "contact" looked clickable and wasn't — found by actually locating the rendered anchor in a browser, not by trusting the source text.
